# i-Genomics: A Genetic Testing Industry Case Study

**Industry analysis · Business model reasoning · Cross-disciplinary innovation**

[繁體中文](README.md) · [Case study](docs/case-study.md) · [Industry structure](docs/industry-analysis.md) · [Business model](docs/business-model.md)

How can a genetic testing business combine biomedical expertise, information engineering and management to turn laboratory data into an understandable service?

This case starts from a 31-slide team presentation for *Cross-disciplinary Innovation and Management*. The user identified i-Genomics, a Taiwanese genetic testing company, as the subject. The repository develops the original material into an industry and company analysis. Detailed chapters are in Traditional Chinese.

**Portfolio curation: Eason Li, October 2026.** The historical presentation credits eight team members, including Eason. Individual slide ownership is not recorded. The new analysis was prepared with AI assistance; it is not presented as historical fieldwork or a company-endorsed assessment.

## Central argument

The proposed source of value is the connection between testing, interpretation and service delivery. Product breadth alone cannot establish customer value or competitive advantage.

The original presentation discusses health risk assessment, cancer companion diagnostics, aging-related tracking and a gene bank. These categories should be analyzed separately because their intended uses and evidence requirements differ. This case does not infer one product's effectiveness from another.

Three hypotheses guide the analysis:

1. Better handoffs between sample collection, analysis, reporting and consultation may create more value than adding product categories.
2. Biomedical, engineering and management teams need shared specifications, review responsibilities and version records to deliver a coherent product.
3. Long-term genomic information services create maintenance obligations as well as opportunities. The available evidence does not establish recurring revenue, customer retention or network effects.

## Evidence and limits

The presentation is historical team coursework; the course year is unconfirmed. Company pages were consulted on 28 October 2026 as first-party descriptions. These sources do not establish audited operating performance. No new interviews, customer surveys, clinical evaluation or internal system inspection were performed.

The original Chinese title uses a fortune-telling metaphor. It is not a claim that genetic tests predict an individual's future with certainty. See the [FDA's consumer explanation](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/in-vitro-diagnostics/direct-consumer-tests) for the distinction between genetic and overall disease risk. US guidance is used for conceptual context, not as a finding about Taiwanese regulatory compliance.

The complete source PDF remains local because it includes a sample testing report and QR codes. Three reviewed slides illustrate the team's process and knowledge mapping. No individual genetic results are published. [Provenance](docs/provenance.md), [attribution](docs/contribution.md) and [references](docs/references.md) explain the boundaries.

No market-size estimate, financial return, clinical benefit or implemented improvement is claimed.
